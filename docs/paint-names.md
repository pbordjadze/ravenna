# The paints' names

Paul, October 28: "the paints should have their own names that'd be cool" (issue #16). Each
mosaic's paints are named after the real materials and colours of Roman and Byzantine mosaic and
wall painting, and a few materials of their world (Egyptian faience, the Fayum portraits'
limewood and linen, Byzantine enamel): the stones and marbles of the floors and walls, the pigments of the painters
(Pliny, Vitruvius, Theophilus, the Byzantine painters' manual, the analyses of the Fayum
portraits) and the glass, metals and ceramics of the tesserae. The list's one source is
`docs/paint-names.json`; `tools/paint_names.py` writes from it `PaintNames.table` and
`PaintNames.credits` (`Sources/MosaicCore/PaintNamesTable.swift`, in this page's order: the names
and their credits in Settings › Acknowledgements) and the list below. How the app uses it is in
`App/CLAUDE.md` › The paints' names.

## How a paint gets its name

PaintCore's own draw (`ColorNickname.assign(_:seed:vocabulary:)`): each paint draws one of the
materials nearest its colour, seeded by the artwork, unique within the palette. PaintCore reaches
as far as ΔE 0.12 in OKLab; Ravenna keeps a drawn name only within 0.08
(`PaintNames.maximumDistance`, about four just-noticeable differences). A paint whose draw
landed farther (the draw can land past the cutoff, or move a paint to a farther material when its
own are taken) gets, in palette order, the nearest material within 0.08 that no other paint has;
a paint with none keeps its structured name ("Black", "Vivid blue") rather than a stretched one.

- **The library** (27 mosaics: 9 works, 3 tile counts, over 4 seeds): 2264 of 2312 paints named
  (97.9%). The 48 left are pure black (#000000), which no material comes near.
- **Ordinary photos** (66 mosaics from 33 test photos: 20 Kodak, 7 scikit-image, Paint by
  Moonlight's 6 corpus photos, at the standard and the larger tile count; contours from an edge
  map, not HED): 99.6% of paints named. The engine's palettes are muted (tile colours are photo
  medians; the most saturated reached chroma 0.205), so few paints are far from every material.
  The 15 entries at the end of the list (Topazos to Conchyliatum) were added for them (October 28),
  where the first 174 left vivid yellow-greens, bright oranges, greyish blues and pale mints
  with stretched names. Bright neon colours, pure blue and pure magenta have no ancient material
  and keep their structured names.

Paint by Moonlight's vocabulary, by comparison, names every paint, with nicknames like
"Cappuccino".

## How the entries were chosen

Names follow the rule for credits in docs/making-of.md: every name is the material's name as a
source gives it (English, the Latin of Pliny, Vitruvius and Theophilus, the Greek of the painters'
manual, or the marble workers' Italian of the Corsi collection), and every source was fetched and
read (October 2026). Nothing here adds a place or a history the source does not state.
Confidence: **H** is a primary text, museum record or peer-reviewed paper read directly; **M** is a
reputable secondary source (ColourLex, a glossary quoted by a mosaic school).

The colours are readings, not measurements of the ancient material:

- **Stones** mostly from the Corsi collection's specimen photographs (Oxford University Museum
  of Natural History; the mean of a central crop) or The Met's object photographs (the median of
  the object), with the colour Corsi or Pliny describes. A stone of several colours (a breccia)
  takes its overall tone.
- **Pigments** from the source's description of the colour and ColourLex's swatches; no source
  read gives numbers. A few readings sit on a colour of the library's paintings, within what the
  description allows.
- **Glass and ceramics** from The Met's photographs of Byzantine tesserae and objects, the Getty
  glossary's photographs and published figures. The Met's tesserae are not chemically analysed:
  pairing a chemistry (cuprite, cobalt) with one of them rests on the Ravenna papers' chemistry
  and a look-alike photograph.

Six readings were moved so that a colour word in the name matches the swatch (PaintCore's
style check): Vine Black, Spanish Green, Red Lead, Yellow Ochre, Tyrian Purple and Opaque White,
each noted below.

Readings to know: Basanites is matched to Corsi's Bekhen stone by this research, not by either
source; Lucullan Marble's colour is Pliny's "entirely black" with no image; Gagates is read from a
medieval jet pendant; Stannum from a silver plate; Lime Mortar from a modern repair mortar; Shell
stands for mother-of-pearl (two hyphens break the name rules). Of the 15 added for photos: Weld
Green and Hysginum are computed mixes of the list's own colours (caeruleum dyed with weld; kermes
under purple); Pyrochlore is the modern mineral name of the opacifier in yellow-green mosaic glass;
which of a Coptic lot of beads are Curalium's coral is this research's reading; Glastum and Vitrum
are both woad, light and deep.

The table is frozen once it ships: its order takes part in the seeded draw, so a change is a new
table with a version recorded per artwork.

- **Atramentum** `#1C1A18`: Roman black pigment, made chiefly from the soot of burnt resin or pitch (best from torch-pine); one of Apelles' four colors. Source: Pliny, Natural History 35.25 <https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=35:chapter=25> (H): Polygnotus and Micon ... made their black from grape-husks, and called it "tryginon." Colour: warm brownish black, as charred grape residue.
- **Elephantinon** `#1E1914`: Black Apelles made from burnt ivory. Source: Pliny, Natural History 35.25 <https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=35:chapter=25> (H): Apelles invented a method of preparing it from burnt ivory, the name given to it being "elephantinon." - **Charcoal Black** `#100E10`: Carbon black from charred wood; found in the eyes, hair and background of a Fayum encaustic portrait.
